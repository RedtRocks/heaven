# Keepsake: idea document

Working name. Change it before anything goes public.

## One line

A personal memory assistant you build about yourself, every day, that can later become a voice and video version of you that your family can talk to, only if you choose.

## The problem

People lose most of what they said, thought and lived through. Diaries die after two weeks. Photos keep faces but not how someone talked, what they believed, or the stories they told at dinner.

When someone dies, families are left with voicemails and a few videos. Existing "talk to your loved one" products mostly rebuild a person from scraps after death, without that person ever agreeing to it. The results feel wrong, and they invent things the person never said.

## The product

Keepsake gives value in two stages.

**Stage 1, for the user, now.** The user feeds Keepsake their day. That can be voice notes, journal entries, chat exports, or transcripts from a wearable recorder like Limitless, Bee or Plaud. Every night Keepsake turns the raw input into structured memories. The user can then ask things like "what did Rohan and I decide about the trip?", "what was I stressed about in March?" or "summarize my month." This is the reason people keep using it.

**Stage 2, for the family, later.** After enough data builds up, the user can record voice samples and face video to create a clone of themselves. The clone talks in their style and answers only from their real memories. The user decides who gets access, what those people can see, and what happens after death. They can revoke access at any time while alive.

The legacy clone is a result of stage 1, not a separate chore. Nobody has to "work on their afterlife." They use a memory assistant, and the archive grows by itself.

## How a user moves through it

1. Sign up and verify identity.
2. Connect or upload daily sources. Voice notes, text, wearable exports.
3. Keepsake processes each day into memories and tags each one with a privacy level.
4. The user reviews, edits or deletes memories whenever they want.
5. The user chats with their own memory assistant.
6. Optional. The user records a consent statement, voice samples and face video to build their clone.
7. Optional. The user invites family members and sets what each one can access.
8. Optional. The user names a digital executor and writes rules for after death, for example "my kids get access, delete everything after 10 years."

## Non-negotiable principles

These are the product. Break one and the idea turns into a deepfake tool.

1. **Only you can create a clone of you.** No uploading someone else's face, voice or data to build their clone. Clone creation requires a live, recorded consent statement from the account owner.
2. **The clone never invents memories.** It answers from stored memories. If nothing matches, it says it doesn't remember or never talked about that.
3. **Private stays private.** Every memory has a privacy level. The clone will not reveal a private memory to anyone, including family.
4. **Other people's words are protected.** Wearables record everyone. Keepsake stores what other people said as a short summary, not as verbatim quotes, and the user can exclude a person entirely.
5. **Access can be revoked.** The owner can remove any family member's access, or delete everything, at any time.
6. **After death, the owner's written rules decide.** A nominated executor carries them out. Keepsake does not make those choices.
7. **Your data can leave.** Full export at any time, so the archive survives even if the company doesn't.
8. **No design that profits from grief.** No streaks, no "she misses you" notifications, no pushing grieving users to spend more time with the clone. Session limits and a clear path to real support if a user seems to be in crisis.

## What it is not

It is not a way to bring back someone who never signed up. It is not a companion or a romantic partner. It is not therapy.

## Existing products and how this differs

HereAfter AI and StoryFile record interviews with living people, which is close to this idea, but they depend on sit-down interview sessions rather than daily life data. Project December and similar tools rebuild dead people from text the family supplies, without the person's consent. Limitless and Bee capture daily life as memory assistants but have no legacy or family-sharing layer.

Keepsake combines a daily memory assistant with consent-first legacy sharing. The daily use keeps people engaged. The consent rules make the legacy part defensible.

## Risks and open questions

- **Retention.** Stage 1 has to be useful enough that people feed it daily for years. This is the biggest risk, and the MVP exists to test it.
- **Emotional harm.** The research on whether talking to a clone helps or blocks grieving is mixed. Watch how families use it and be willing to limit usage.
- **Legal.** India's DPDP Act covers the personal data. Indian courts, the Delhi High Court in particular, have been protecting personality rights over voice and likeness. Get legal advice before stage 2 launches.
- **Third-party consent.** Recording laws for bystanders differ by country.
- **Security.** This will be one of the most sensitive databases a person has. A breach is catastrophic.
- **Longevity.** If the company shuts down, families lose their person a second time. Export and a long-term storage plan are required, not optional.

## Roadmap

- **Phase 1, memory assistant.** Upload voice notes and text, nightly processing into memories, chat with your own memories, privacy levels, edit and delete. Single user.
- **Phase 2, your voice.** Voice cloning from owner-recorded samples, real-time voice conversation with your own memory assistant.
- **Phase 3, sharing.** Family invites, access tiers, revocation, executor, after-death rules, export.
- **Phase 4, avatar.** Talking-head video from owner-recorded face video.

## What success looks like for phase 1

The builder uses it on themselves every day for four weeks. Five to ten friends use it for two weeks, and at least half keep adding data without being reminded. Answers pulled from memories are correct and nothing gets invented in a manual review of 100 questions.
